I read and listen to a fair amount, and since mid-2025 I've kept real notes on it.
The process is simple. I jot down rough notes while I'm going, then dictate a messy
stream of thoughts into an AI and go back and forth until the themes actually match
what I took away. It's turned into the most useful thing I do with these tools.

Here's what stuck from the first half of 2026. I rate everything out of 10. An 8 or
above means I'd recommend it, a 7 means read it if the topic grabs you, and 6 or
below means skip it. These are the 8s and up.

## Greenlights — 9/10

Matthew McConaughey kept diaries for 35 years and built a book out of them. His
argument is that good luck isn't really luck. You do the unglamorous work first,
and sometimes you have to walk away from something comfortable before the good
thing shows up.

The part I keep coming back to is the documenting itself. He wrote down the ugly
stuff too, and that's where most of the lessons came from.

## Benjamin Franklin: An American Life — 8/10

Two things stuck. Franklin tracked 13 virtues in a 13-column grid notebook and
worked on one per week. That's a habit tracker in the 1730s.

The other is that after reading Xenophon he basically stopped arguing with people.
He'd ask questions until they talked themselves into his position. He also wrote
*Poor Richard's Almanack* in plain, funny language because that's what regular
people would actually read — a lesson I'm apparently still learning.

## Billion Dollar Whale — 8/10

The Jho Low and 1MDB story. The detail that got me: he registered a company in the
British Virgin Islands called "Aabar Investments PJS Limited." The real Abu Dhabi
fund was called "Aabar Investments PJS." One word apart. That was enough to move
billions past compliance checks at serious institutions.

Goldman underwrote $6.5 billion of bonds on the deal and took around $600 million
in fees. When the money is that good, nobody wants to look too closely.

## The Changing World Order — 8/10

Ray Dalio's argument is that countries run the same cycle over and over. They get
productive, they get rich, they borrow, they can't pay it back, they print money,
and the people holding the debt get wiped out. He scores national power on eight
different measures that tend to move in a predictable order.

It's repetitive, and he'll tell you the same thing four times. The framework is
still worth having.

## The New Map — 8/10

Daniel Yergin on energy and geopolitics. One number reframed a lot for me: fossil
fuels made up 84% of world energy in 2019, which is exactly what they made up 20
years earlier. All that solar and wind growth was addition, not replacement.

Also worth knowing — the US imported 60% of its oil in 2008, and by 2018 it had
passed both Russia and Saudi Arabia to become the largest producer in the world.

## My Life in Full — 8/10

Indra Nooyi. The distinction I took from it is between a mentor and a sponsor. A
mentor gives you advice. A sponsor spends their own credibility to move you up, and
her career turned on the second kind.

She's also blunt that the superwoman story is nonsense. It worked because of a
multi-generational support system at home and a husband who took a back seat.

## Acquired — Trader Joe's — 8/10

A normal supermarket carries about 50,000 products. Trader Joe's carries about
4,000. That one constraint drives everything else. They buy enormous volume of a
single good version of each thing, so it's cheap, and they never have to compete on
selection.

Joe Coulombe deliberately built a model 7-Eleven couldn't copy without wrecking its
own business. There are no dedicated cashiers either — a bell rings and whoever's
stocking shelves jumps on a register.

[Listen here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sof42S1icXg)

## Acquired — Indian Premier League — 8/10

Lalit Modi sold roughly $1 billion of TV rights to Sony before a single ball was
bowled. That meant franchise owners got paid before they had to spend anything,
which solves the hardest problem with starting any new league.

Then he capped it at eight teams and ran an auction, so India's billionaires had to
compete for something deliberately scarce. Shah Rukh Khan paid $75 million for
Kolkata.

[Listen here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h6FS70B0blQ)

to you.
